# When to ask a person: the conditions for stopping

## Learning objective

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to recognise the three conditions that mean steering has stopped working, hand the problem to a person in the same three parts you would hand it to your agent — what you saw, what you asked for, what came back, and nothing pasted — and treat stopping as one of the moves rather than the end of them.

## Why this matters

There is a particular hour that everybody working this way eventually gets. The same problem, described a fourth way, to an agent that is still confident and still wrong; the afternoon gone; and a quiet private suspicion that somebody who knew what they were doing would have had this finished before lunch. Everything in this course so far has been a way of getting unstuck on your own, and it works nearly all of the time — which is exactly what makes the rest of the time hard to recognise from inside it. What is missing is the other half of the skill: knowing which hours are the ones to stop, and what to carry to somebody else when you do.

## Core read

Nothing gets built here, nothing gets repaired, and there is nothing to run at the end. This is the one lesson in the module that leaves your app completely alone.

What it gives you instead is three conditions and a short handover — and the reason those are worth a lesson of their own is that both are hardest to see from inside the hour they apply to.

### The three conditions

None of them is about how hard the problem is. That is the first useful thing about them: difficulty is a feeling, it arrives early and often, and it is a terrible signal. These three are things you can count.

**You have restated the same thing three times.** Module 3 gave you the steer and told you the truth about it — two or three rounds is normal, and the fix is often a tighter version of what you already said. It also gave you the move for when tightening stops helping: start a fresh conversation and say it again from the beginning. This condition sits one step past that. You restated, you started fresh, you restated again in the new conversation, and what came back was recognisably the same answer. At that point the problem is no longer a wording problem, and another wording is not going to reach it.

**"Done" keeps arriving and the check keeps failing.** Module 4 gave you the sentence for work reported finished with nothing to show for it, and Lesson 2 of this module had you write the definition of done into the ask so that finishing meant demonstrating. This condition is what is left when both of those are working properly: the report arrives, the results are there, and then you open the app and the thing still does not do what you meant. Once is ordinary. Three times is a pattern, and the pattern is that you and your agent are not disagreeing about the work — you are disagreeing about what the words mean, and neither of you can see where.

**What is at stake is money, other people's information, or something you cannot take back.** This one is the odd one out, and the difference matters. The first two are earned: you tried, and the trying is what told you. This one applies before you have tried anything, and it does not get less true by going carefully. You already have a question for the moment your agent proposes a single step that cannot be undone. This is the wider case — where the whole situation carries that weight, and the person who pays for a wrong call is not you.

### The condition that is not on the list

"I do not understand what it is doing" is not one of them.

You have never understood what it is doing. That has been the arrangement since Module 2, it is the reason you got this far this fast, and it has held through a deploy, a live app, two accounts, a repair, and an addition. Not understanding the machinery is the normal state of this work, not a warning light. Feeling out of your depth is not a condition; being unable to move is.

### What to bring

Somebody who can help will ask you three questions, and they are the three you have been answering all course:

- **What you saw** — the actual behaviour, on the actual screen, in the app you opened.
- **What you asked for** — what you said you wanted, in the words you used.
- **What came back** — what happened instead, and how many times.

That is the shape of every steer you have written since Module 3, pointed at a person instead of an agent. You are not learning a new format today; you are noticing that you already have one.

Then the part that is easier to get wrong. **Do not bring code, error text, or anything else your agent produced.** Two reasons, and the second is the one people miss.

The first is that you have not read it. You cannot vouch for it, you cannot say where it came from, and you cannot answer the first question anybody will ask you about it.

The second is that what you paste decides which question you have asked. Hand somebody a description and you have asked *can you help me with this problem*. Hand them a screenful of code and you have asked *can you review this* — a different favour, a much larger one, and one you cannot take part in. They will answer in its terms, and you will be exactly where you started with somebody else's evening spent as well. Lesson 3 had you describe rather than paste for a reason about what pasted text can carry with it. This is the same discipline for an entirely different reason: pasting changes the question.

### Who to ask

Three honest answers, in the order most people find them.

Somebody who builds software — and it does not have to be somebody who has ever seen the kind of app you built. The conditions above are not about what your app is made of; a person who has been stuck in a loop with a tool will recognise all three instantly.

The people already standing next to the project. The person who reported the fault can tell you what they did in a way you cannot get from the app. Whoever else uses it can tell you whether they see it too. That is not the same help as a developer, and on the first two conditions it is often better, because it goes at the disagreement about what the words mean.

And the third answer, which is the real one for most people reading this: you may not have anybody yet. Notice that on a calm afternoon rather than a bad one. Finding the person is a job for a day when nothing is broken, and it is a considerably easier job then.

### Stopping is a move

The loop has four moves in it, and *keep going* has never been one of them.

Say plainly what the fourth restatement costs, because it is easy to lose track from inside. It costs the hour. It costs the next hour, because the answer that comes back will be near enough to the last one to be worth trying. And it costs a bit of your appetite for the whole project, which is the expensive one and the one nobody counts.

Against that, the thing that keeps people going is rarely a belief that the next message will work. It is the feeling that stopping is an admission. It is worth knowing that the people who do this for a living ask each other things constantly, all day, and that asking is their ordinary operating mode rather than their emergency one. The unusual part of your position is not that you get stuck. It is that you have fewer people to ask — and that is a fixable thing, on a different day, in advance.

The opposite mistake is real too, which is what the conditions are for. Stopping at the first sign of difficulty means never finding out that the second attempt usually works. Three restatements, three failed checks, or something that cannot be taken back. Not a mood, and not a mystery.

## Exercise

Nothing runs and nothing changes. The deliverable is one written handover and one name — or, honestly, one place to look for a name. About fifteen minutes.

1. **Pick a real one.** The longest you have been stuck anywhere in this course, or on your own project since. It does not have to have ended badly, and it is more useful if it did not.
2. **Write the handover from memory,** in three short parts: what you saw, what you asked for, what came back. Write it for somebody who has never opened your app.
3. **Count the rounds.** How many times did you say the same thing in different words before something moved? Write the number down next to it. That number is the one you will want next time, and it is the one nobody remembers afterwards.
4. **Read it back and cut anything that is not one of the three parts** — especially anything you would have copied out of your agent's replies.
5. **Name your person.** Somebody who builds things, somebody using your app, or a place you would go looking if you had to today. If the honest answer is that you do not have one, write that down as it is. It is the most useful line on the page.
6. **Keep it with what you wrote down in Lessons 1 to 3.** Four lessons, four written things, one place to find them.

## Checkpoint

You've got this if you can do both:

1. Name the three conditions without looking, and say which one of them applies before you have tried anything — and why that one is different from the other two.
2. Say the three parts of what you bring to a person, and give the two reasons you leave your agent's code out of it.

## Going deeper

Optional, only if you're curious:

- The three conditions are not about software. Repeating yourself to something that will not move, being told a thing is finished when it plainly is not, and standing in front of a decision whose cost lands on somebody else — those turn up in any work where you are directing something you cannot do yourself. The counting is what makes them usable; the feeling on its own never has been.
- Module 7 — Where to go from here — is next, and it is the last one. It covers session hygiene: when to start fresh, when to switch to a different agent, and when the right move is to leave the keyboard entirely — plus where to take this once the course runs out. Nothing to prepare.

## Loop check

> **Loop check — intent.** This lesson is **intent**, which is where the loop started and the only one of its four moves that has never needed an agent. Ask, evaluate and steer all happen in a conversation; take the conversation away and none of them exists. Intent is the one that lives in you — the thing you were trying to do, held before anything was asked and unchanged by however badly the asking went. That is precisely why it is the move you come back to when the other three have stopped working. You are not writing a better ask. You are going back to what you actually wanted, and carrying it to somebody who can hold it with you. Which makes stopping the loop's first move rather than its failure, and it is the reason this lesson has no check in it: nothing here needs testing, because the thing you are recovering was never in the app.

## What you just did

You wrote the handover you would give a person, in the same three parts you have been handing to your agent since Module 3, and you either named somebody to give it to or found out that you have not got one yet. That closes Module 6. You can take a fault somebody else reported and turn it into a repair you can check; you have added something to a live app on purpose and proved the rest of it survived; you have watched text from outside a conversation try to steer your agent and stopped it at the approval prompt; and you now know the three conditions under which the right answer is not another message. Module 5 left you able to hold an app steady. This module was about changing it without losing that — and this last lesson is the part that keeps you in the work long enough for any of it to matter.
