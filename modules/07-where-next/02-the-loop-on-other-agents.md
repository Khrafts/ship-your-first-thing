# The loop on other agents

## Learning objective

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to name which parts of the way you work belong to the loop rather than to the app you learned it in, say what changes when the app changes, and name the five questions that locate each of those parts in an agent app you have never opened.

## Why this matters

Lesson 1 handed you three moves that survive a change of tool, and the middle one was moving to a different agent. That move is easy to say and expensive to feel, because everything you know how to do you learned inside one window — where the approval prompt appears, how your agent talks back, where the work lands when it is finished. Open something else and none of that furniture is where you left it, and for the first ten minutes it is genuinely hard to tell whether you have lost your skill or only your bearings. What you are missing is the thing that tells those two apart: a short, honest list of what was never about the app in the first place.

> **Following along:** Nothing in this module runs — no build, no repair and no check — and nothing here touches your app. The app you finished Module 6 with is the app you keep, exactly as you left it. There is also nothing to install for this lesson: it names other agent apps, and it does not ask you to go and get one.

## Core read

Six modules of practice, and almost none of it was about the app.

That is easy to write and hard to believe from inside the only window you have ever used, so here is the specific version. What follows is a **translation key** (the mapping from the loop you learned in Module 3 to AI coding agents this course does not teach, [→ GLOSSARY](../../GLOSSARY.md#translation-key)): a list in two halves — what stays put when the app changes, and what moves.

### The half that stays put

[Module 3](../03-the-loop/README.md) named the loop and then ran it, one move at a time, on a practice page: knowing what you want, asking for it, looking at what came back, saying what should be different. The first four below are those moves in working clothes; the three after them are the habits the course built around them. Not one of the seven mentions an app.

- **You decide what you want before you type.** The sharper that sentence is, the less of the rest of this list you need — true of a tool nobody has built yet.
- **You ask for the outcome and leave the method alone.** "Add today's date below the tagline" rather than a description of how to work out what today is. Any agent is better at the how than you are, and that is the arrangement rather than a limitation.
- **You look at the running thing.** Your browser is where you check; the agent's own words are the second signal. Reading what it wrote was never your job, and it does not become your job in a different window.
- **You name what you saw and what should be different.** One sentence, no apology, no starting over. That move is the same two halves in any window, and it is the one that gets used most.
- **You approve or you decline, one change at a time.** The pause before anything touches your machine is the one control this whole course is built on.
- **You say "save this as a working version" when something works.** The sentence is yours; the machinery underneath belongs to the agent. That is the shape of an ask, not a feature of an app.
- **You start a fresh conversation when the conversation is the problem.** Lesson 1 gave you the condition. It is a move you make, not a thing the app does for you.

The limits travel too. This course did not measure them app by app, and it does not need to: all three belong to the agent rather than to the window around it. An agent has a fixed amount of room to hold a conversation in, so long sessions get worse and the fresh-conversation move is not a quirk of the one you learned — [Module 3 Lesson 2](../03-the-loop/02-planning-vs-execution.md) has the smell-test. An agent writes with the same easy confidence whether it is right or wrong, so a confident answer is never evidence of a correct one — [Module 3 Lesson 3](../03-the-loop/03-reading-plans-recognizing-wrong.md) is where you were armed for that. And an agent will offer you the thing that cannot be undone in the even voice it uses for a spelling fix — the question [Module 2 Lesson 1](../02-toolchain/01-your-ai-coding-agent.md) taught you to ask at the approval prompt. None of the three is a fault of a particular app, so expect all three rather than hoping they are local — your answer to each travels either way.

### The half that moves

Everything else — which sounds like a lot until you see the list:

- **The name.** The app's, and whoever makes it.
- **The shape of the window.** Where you type, where the reply lands, whether it is its own app or a mode inside a larger one — you have already met both kinds.
- **What the approval moment looks like.** Where it appears, how it words the question, and whether there is a setting that changes how often it asks. The pause is the durable part; the dialog is not.
- **What it calls starting over.** A button, a menu item, a new tab down the side — the same move under a different label.
- **What it costs, and what happens when you reach the end of what it gives you.** [Module 0 Lesson 3](../00-welcome/03-cost-path-triage.md) priced that for the two apps here. Nothing on that page transfers to a third one — only the habit of finding out before you need to.
- **Where the work lands on your computer**, and whether the app tells you without being asked.
- **The personality.** Module 3 shows the same ask in both apps, and the two replies are not shaped alike: one volunteers the limits of what it did and names what to go and look at, the other does the job and offers to do more. That difference is not a ranking, and the loop underneath is identical.

### Five questions for an app you have never opened

Most of these you can answer in the first ten minutes, and most by asking the agent itself — which is, appropriately, the first ask you make of it.

1. **Where does it stop and check with me?** Have it change something small and watch for the pause. If nothing ever stops to ask, you are not in the arrangement this course taught you — not a verdict on the app, but a different deal, and worth knowing you have taken it.
2. **Where does the work land?** Ask it plainly: which folder on this computer, and what is it called. You want to be able to find your own work without the app's help.
3. **What is the running thing, and where do I look at it?** In this course the answer was always a browser tab. Get the equivalent open before you ask for anything real — it is where the evaluate move happens.
4. **What do I say to save a working version, and how do I get back to one?** Say your sentence and see what it does. The answer to the second half is the one that matters on a bad afternoon.
5. **How do I start a fresh conversation?** Find it before you need it. Hour three is a poor time to go hunting through a menu.

Answer those five and you have translated the first half of this lesson into a window you have never seen. Nothing else here is load-bearing.

### What this course can and cannot tell you

Two things past the two tracks you were taught are worth naming here, and that is this module's job.

**OpenCode desktop** you have met by name already, when you picked a path in Module 0 and again in Module 2. Nothing this course says about it has changed: free and genuinely capable, the least user-friendly of the three, built for people who are comfortable finding their own way, and this course has not verified how it saves your work. Set that last clause next to question 4 above and you can see why it matters more than it sounds.

**Agents that live inside a code editor** are the other, and this course names them as a category and never by product. A code editor is the window developers keep code in, and there are agents that work from inside one. The names in that category change faster than anyone can re-check them, so a list here would be wrong before you read it — and the arrangement is a different one from yours. Your surfaces in this course have been the agent app, your browser and your phone; a window full of code is not one of them. The translation key still applies — the moves in the first half are the moves — but you would be doing them somewhere this course never put you.

Now the caveat, and it is the reason this page is a key rather than a review. Of the two apps this course teaches, it built all the way through one and installed the other and made a first edit with it. It has not worked in OpenCode desktop and it has not worked in an agent that lives inside a code editor, so it cannot tell you how either behaves, what either costs, or whether either is any good. The claim here is narrower than it might have sounded: those seven lines are what working with an agent is made of — a claim about the shape of the work, not a promise about any particular app. A course that let you read it as a promise would be handing you something nobody checked.

There is also no recommendation on this page. Lesson 1 already gave you the two conditions under which switching is the move, and the one under which you skip it. That is the whole of the advice; this lesson only says what you would be carrying with you.

The app you learned in is the least durable thing in this arrangement. It will be redesigned, renamed, repriced, or replaced by something nobody has announced yet, and the seven lines above will still be the seven lines. That is the part you actually own.

## Exercise

Nothing runs. The deliverable is one page you keep — the moves, the five questions, and one line at the bottom. About fifteen minutes.

1. **Write the half that stays put in your own words,** one short line for each of the seven, with no app name and no button name in any of them. If a line only makes sense when you mention a window, a menu or a tab, that one is a habit you have been carrying rather than a move you own — rewrite it until it is about you and the work.
2. **Write the five questions in your own words,** and next to each, write the answer for the app you already use. That half you can fill in now, from memory, and it should take about two minutes. The blank space beside it is the point of the exercise: it is the exact size of the ten minutes a new app would cost you.
3. **Write the one thing you would refuse to work without.** One line. For most people it is the pause before anything changes, but write down whichever it actually is for you, because that is the thing you should check for first and it is easiest to notice you have lost after it is gone.
4. **Keep it with the card from Lesson 1** — same page if you like. Two short cards in one findable place beats two good notes you cannot find.

## Checkpoint

You've got this if you can do both:

1. Name the parts of your working method that stay the same when the agent app changes, and say which one you would notice missing first.
2. Say what this course has verified about the agents it names but does not teach, and what it has not.

## Going deeper

Optional, only if you're curious:

- Module 3 already set this side by side in front of you and you may not have noticed. Every exchange in all four lessons is shown twice — once in each app — and across the whole module the loop does not change once, while the wording, the dialogs and the shape of the replies change constantly. It is worth one skim with that in mind, rather than the skim you gave it while trying to get your own page working.
- A translation key has an honest limit worth naming: it tells you what to look for, not what you will find. If question 1 comes back with an answer you do not like, the key has done its whole job — it got you to the question early, while the answer was still cheap.

## Loop check

> **Loop check — ask.** All four moves come up on this page, but the one it turns on is *ask*. What survives a change of tool is the shape of what you say: an outcome rather than a method, one thing at a time, said in your own words. The window around it is the part that gets redrawn, and the five questions are themselves asks — the first ones you would make of an app you have never opened, aimed at the app rather than at the work.

## What you just did

You wrote down the moves that belong to you rather than to the app you learned them in, put five questions next to them for a window you have not seen yet, and named the one thing you would refuse to work without. That is the second of the things this module says you keep: the session moves from Lesson 1, and now the loop itself. Which leaves exactly one question, and Lesson 3 is that question — the loop travels, so what is worth pointing it at next, and what is honestly not worth your afternoon.
