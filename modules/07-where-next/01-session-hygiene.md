# Session hygiene: starting fresh, switching, and stopping

## Learning objective

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to name the three moves that act on the working session rather than on the work — starting a fresh conversation, moving to a different agent, and leaving the keyboard — say which condition selects each one, and say what each of them is a permanent answer to rather than a beginner's workaround.

## Why this matters

You have already met two of these three moves and never had a name for either. Module 3 had you start a fresh conversation, at the point where restating stops helping. Module 6 gave you the conditions under which the next move is a person rather than another message — and shutting the laptop on a bad afternoon, then finding the answer sitting there in the morning, is something everybody who works at anything hard has done. What you have not had is a name for them as one thing, or a way of telling which of the three you are standing in, so the cheapest one gets used for all three jobs and the other two get reached for late, after the afternoon has already gone.

> **Following along:** Nothing in this module runs — no build, no repair and no check — and nothing here touches your app. The app you finished Module 6 with is the app you keep, exactly as you left it. What this lesson gives you is three moves, named, with the conditions that tell them apart.

## Core read

> **Deviation note:** This read runs longer than most in the course. Three moves get named here and each one is only usable with the condition that selects it and the thing it permanently answers attached — split those apart and you get three labels the learner cannot act on.

The loop has four moves in it and every one of them points at the work: what you want, what you ask for, what came back, what should be different. Six modules of practice, all of it aimed at the thing on the screen.

The three moves in this lesson point somewhere else. None of them changes a line of anything. They change the conditions you are working in — the conversation you are having, the tool you are having it with, or whether you are at the desk at all. **Session hygiene** is the name for that: housekeeping on the session rather than on the work. It has real timing, and it is not the set of things you do once you have given up.

One claim holds all three together, and it is worth having before the details: each is the standing answer to something that does not improve with your skill. Not a beginner's workaround a better operator would not need — the better operator reaches for them sooner and more cheaply, and that is the whole of the difference.

### Move one — start a fresh conversation

You know this one. [Module 3 Lesson 2](../03-the-loop/02-planning-vs-execution.md) gave you the picture, the smell-test and the move: your agent has a fixed amount of room to hold a conversation in — the **context window** — and when the room runs out, older parts of what you said stop being there. What you feel from outside is **drift**: replies that stay fluent and confident while quietly being about something you did not ask for. The smell-test and the two-try limit both live in that lesson, and nothing here replaces them.

What this lesson adds is where the move sits next to the other two. **Start fresh when the problem is the conversation.** That is the whole condition. Something you ruled out has come back; an instruction from forty minutes ago has quietly stopped applying; the session feels muddy and you cannot say why. In each of those, what stands between you and the result is the record of what has already been said — so you replace the record, not the tool and not the day.

What it is a permanent answer to: the room is finite in every one of these tools, and it will still be finite when you are fast at this. Long conversations will always end up worse than short ones, and the fix is not to get better at long conversations.

### Move two — move to a different agent

This is the one you have never done, and people either never think of it or reach for it far too early. Two conditions, unrelated to each other.

**A fresh conversation reached the same wall, the same way, twice.** You started over, said it plainly from the top, and the second run failed exactly where the first did. You have already replaced the conversation, so the conversation was not the problem. Putting the same ask in front of the other app is the cheapest way left to find out whether the wall is the tool's or yours.

**Your app is not available or not affordable today.** It is down, or you have used up whatever your path gives you for the day, and the work is not going to wait. [Module 0 Lesson 3](../00-welcome/03-cost-path-triage.md) said this out loud when you picked a path: nothing locks you to the path you picked, and switching costs you exactly one thing — creating the account you skipped the first time. That price has not changed.

This move has a real failure mode. **Switching is not a way to get a better answer to the same loose ask.** If what you said had no edge on it, the second agent will run off in its own direction as happily as the first one — [Module 3 Lesson 4](../03-the-loop/04-steering-and-recovery.md) put the same bookshelf ask in front of both apps and both of them built a bookshelf nobody wanted. Going shopping for an agent that will read your mind is an expensive way to avoid tightening one sentence. The switch is worth exactly one try, and the useful part is the answer it gives you either way: if the other app clears the wall, the wall was the app's; if it lands in the same place, you have stopped guessing, and "both of them do this, here is what I asked" is the best sentence you can hand the person Module 6 taught you to find.

One condition where you skip this move entirely: when what is at stake is money, other people's information, or something that cannot be taken back. A second agent does not make that situation smaller. That one goes straight to move three.

What it is a permanent answer to: any single tool has a shape, and the shape is not yours to change. Which one is available, affordable and behaving well is a fact about a given afternoon, not a fact about you. Module 3 already showed the part that makes this cheap — you watched all four moves of the loop run in both apps, and they did not change once.

### Move three — leave the keyboard

Two shapes, and the course has taught one of them properly. The first is handing the problem to a person: [Module 6 Lesson 4](../06-after-live/04-when-to-ask-a-person.md) is that move in full — the three conditions that mean steering has stopped working, the three parts of what you carry, and why you leave your agent's output out of it. Go back to it rather than rebuilding it from memory; there is nothing to add here.

The second shape is the one nobody says out loud: stopping for today with nobody to hand it to. Three signals, and you can count all of them.

- The rounds are getting shorter and sharper, and you are typing sentences you would not want read back to you.
- You cannot say in one plain sentence what you are currently trying to do. Not what is broken — what you *want*.
- It is late, and the thing waiting at the approval prompt is the kind of thing you would rather be awake for.

Stopping feels expensive and mostly is not. Your work was never in the conversation — the folder and the files sit where they are however many conversations you start or end — and the conversation was going to be replaced anyway. Say **"save this as a working version"** before you close the lid and you have paid the whole cost of stopping in one sentence. Tomorrow starts in a fresh conversation, which is where the good version of today would have started too.

What it is a permanent answer to: you are the part of this arrangement that gets tired, and none of the tool's speed transfers to you. Nothing in the conversation will raise the hour with you either. That is **risk-blindness** — an agent proposes the thing that cannot be undone in the same even voice it uses for a spelling fix — and [Module 2 Lesson 1](../02-toolchain/01-your-ai-coding-agent.md) gave you the question that catches it. What that question cannot catch is that it is eleven at night. Nothing weighs that except you.

### Telling them apart

Sort them by price and the mistake becomes obvious. A fresh conversation costs the conversation. A different agent costs one account and an hour of unfamiliarity. Leaving the keyboard costs the rest of today.

So people reach for the first one over and over, because it is the only price they actually know — and four fresh conversations in a row cost far more than the one switch or the one early night would have. The rule that fixes it is short enough to remember at hour three: **a move you have already made twice is not the move.** Two fresh conversations that landed in the same place mean the conversation was never the problem. Two agents that landed in the same place mean the tool was never the problem, and what is left is a person or a morning.

### Why none of this goes away

Look at what the three moves are answering. A record of the conversation that fills up. A tool whose shape you did not choose. A person who gets tired. Exactly one of those is about AI at all, and even that one is really about any medium where the record of what you have already said becomes the thing you are working through.

Here is the property underneath all three, and it is the reason they travel. When a tool answers the moment you act, the thing that normally stops you is gone. Waiting is what makes people look up, re-read what they asked for and notice they have been going the wrong way — and a tight feedback loop removes it. That speed is why you shipped an app in six modules. It is also why you can spend four hours travelling confidently in the wrong direction with nothing interrupting you. The three moves are the interruptions you now supply yourself.

Which means you already own them everywhere the loop is tight:

- A spreadsheet you have nested formulas into until you can no longer see what it works out. Start a clean sheet; open it in something else; get up.
- A document you have rewritten so many times you can no longer read it the way a stranger would.
- A search you have re-worded eight times, each version closer to the last one than to the question.

Same three moves, same order, and the same mistake of using the cheapest one four times. None of that is about coding, none of it depends on which app you installed in Module 0, and none of it stops being true when that app is replaced by something with a different name. It is the shape of working through anything that answers instantly, and it is the part of this course you will still be using long after the names in it have changed.

## Exercise

Nothing runs and nothing changes. The deliverable is one short card you keep — five lines and two names. About fifteen minutes.

1. **Write the three moves down in your own words,** one line each, and next to each one write the signal *you* will actually notice. Not the one in the lesson — the one you would spot on your own worst afternoon.
2. **Write your number.** How many times will you restate something before you start fresh? Module 3 said two. Pick yours and commit to it in writing, because the number you pick calmly is not the number you will pick at hour three.
3. **Price the switch.** In one line: what would moving to the other app cost you today? Name the account you skipped in Module 0, and write down whether you have it or not. If you do not, you have just found the ten minutes that makes this move available to you later.
4. **Write your stopping sentence.** The one you will say to yourself, and the one you will say to your agent first — "save this as a working version" — so that stopping costs you nothing but the evening.
5. **Name two tools that have nothing to do with AI** where you have gone round in circles: a spreadsheet, a document, a search, a settings screen, whatever it actually was. Next to each, write which of the three moves you eventually made — or which one you wish you had made sooner.
6. **Keep it with the four things Module 6 left you.** Same place, same page if you like — written notes are only useful if they are in one findable spot.

## Checkpoint

You've got this if you can do both:

1. Name the three moves and the condition that selects each one — including the condition under which you skip the middle move entirely and go straight to the third.
2. Say what each of the three is a permanent answer to, and name one tool that has nothing to do with AI where the same three apply.

## Going deeper

Optional, only if you're curious:

- If you want the mechanism under move one rather than the condition, it is already written: [Module 3 Lesson 2](../03-the-loop/02-planning-vs-execution.md) has the notepad picture and the smell-test that goes with it. You do not need it in order to act — the condition is enough — but it is the shortest explanation in the course of why long conversations get worse.
- The three moves are asymmetric in what they save, which is worth noticing once. A fresh conversation keeps the tool and the day and throws away the record. A different agent keeps the record — you can say the same thing again, word for word — and throws away the familiarity. Leaving the keyboard keeps all of it and throws away only the hours you were going to waste. That last one is the only move on the list whose cost is made entirely of things you would have lost anyway.

## Loop check

> **Loop check — steer.** All three of these are steers, one level out. The steer you learned in Module 3 points at the work — what was wrong, what you want, what limit to hold — and it is the move you make when you have looked at what came back and it is not right. These three are the same move pointed at the conditions instead: the conversation is what is wrong, or the tool is, or the hour is, and what should be different is not a line on a page. That is also why this module has no check in it. A check needs something running to be run against, and what you are correcting here was never in the app.
